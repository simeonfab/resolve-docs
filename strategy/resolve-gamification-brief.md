# Resolve — Emotional Weight & Gamification Brief

Status: design direction for future phases, not current scope
Origin: identified as a core retention and differentiation principle during intake flow redesign

---

## The core principle

The real reward Resolve gives users is transformation — arriving with a head full of scattered noise and leaving with one clear next move. That feeling is genuinely powerful. The job of gamification in Resolve is not to add artificial incentives on top of a functional tool — it's to make the transformation itself feel earned, significant, and worth returning for.

Every gamification decision should pass this test: **does this make the clarity feel more meaningful, or does it distract from it?**

---

## What's already implemented (Phase 1)

**Processing reveal** — a 2.5-second animated ring between "bring it in" and "here's what I understood." Makes the AI reading feel like real work happening, not an instant page flip. The reveal of understanding feels earned.

**Win banner (Screen 4)** — personalised to the user's name and their specific project situation. Names what they actually accomplished ("you turned a messy rebrand into a focused path in under 10 minutes") rather than just asking what they want to do next. This is the emotional peak of the current flow.

---

## Phase 2 — When accounts and persistence exist

**Streak mechanic (but not daily — session-based)**
Resolve is not a daily habit app. Don't force daily returns. Instead: streak tracks *sessions completed* — "3 projects clarified," "5 paths built." Each session completion is a win, not each login. The metric is "battles won," not "days active."

**Project milestone log**
A running record of every project a user has brought through Resolve — name, date, what the recommended move was, whether they acted on it. This becomes a personal record of clarity wins. Seeing "6 messy projects resolved" is a powerful retention signal.

**"Path taken" confirmation**
When a user returns to Resolve after acting on a recommended move, they should be able to mark it done — "I did it." A simple confirmation that the recommended move worked (or didn't) closes the loop, creates a sense of accomplishment, and generates real product feedback simultaneously.

---

## Phase 3 — When there's enough usage data

**Clarity score**
A lightweight metric showing how "resolved" a project is over time — based on how many open decisions have been answered, how many gaps have been filled, and whether the recommended move has been acted on. Not a gamified number for its own sake — a genuine signal of project health that improves visibly as the user works through it.

**Unlockable next steps**
Some actions on Screen 4 are locked until the recommended first move is complete. "Write a prototype brief" is greyed out with "complete your first move to unlock this." Creates progression without being punitive — the user always has something to do, but some things are earned.

**"Battle won" moments**
At specific project milestones (first move completed, first tester session done, first paying customer) — a deliberate, full-screen celebration moment. Not a badge or a popup — a proper screen. Big type. Named accomplishment. Specific to what they did. Feels like a game boss cleared, not a notification.

---

## What to never do

- Daily streak pressure ("you'll lose your streak if you don't log in") — Resolve is a clarity tool, not a habit app. Guilt mechanics are wrong for this use case.
- Points or leaderboards — creates comparison between users, which is irrelevant here. Resolve is personal.
- Badges for trivial actions — "first login" badges cheapen the experience. Only milestone-worthy moments get celebration.
- Progress bars on things that aren't actually progress — fake completion metrics undermine the trust that makes Resolve's "honest output" positioning work.

---

## The long-term emotional arc

Session 1: "I just cleared the fog on a messy project. That felt fast and surprisingly good."
Session 3: "I've now resolved 3 different projects with this. It's becoming how I start anything messy."
Session 6+: "This is my system. I trust what it tells me to do first."

Everything in the gamification design should be pointing toward that arc — from "that was surprisingly good" to "this is my system." The win isn't a trophy, it's a habit of clarity.
