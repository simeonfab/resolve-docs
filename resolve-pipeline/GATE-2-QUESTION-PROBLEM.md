# The question we can't get right

**For a fresh reader with no context. Read once, answer the question at the bottom.**

---

## The product

A tool for solo, mostly non-technical founders. They describe a messy project idea in
their own words; the tool produces a roadmap.

Its whole selling point is that it **refuses** to make things up. If what they've told us
genuinely can't support a real roadmap, it stops and asks **one question** instead of
handing over a confident-sounding plan built on nothing. That refusal is the product.

## The gate

Before building a roadmap, the tool checks two things:

**Gate 1 — is there a real outcome?** Something that would actually be *different in the
world* if this worked. Not "the app is built" — that's an artifact. Something like
"clients book their own slots instead of DMing me at 11pm."

**Gate 2 — is there a lever?** Some *ongoing work* the founder could keep doing that keeps
paying off, and that plausibly gets them to that outcome. As opposed to a pile of one-off
tasks ("decide the name", "pick a colour") or a pile of things to build ("build the app,
build the database, build the engine"), from which any roadmap we produced would be
invented by us rather than derived from them.

If either gate fails, we halt and ask one question.

## Gate 1's question — this one works

> **"If this works, what is different in three months that isn't true today?"**

Founders can answer this. Crucially, it asks them to **describe a state of the world** —
an act of imagination, not analysis. They don't need any product-management skill. They
just picture it and tell us.

## Gate 2's question — this one is broken

The current version is:

> **"Of everything that has to go right, which part is yours to decide or change
> directly?"**

Two things are wrong with it.

**1. It's incoherent for the audience.** These are solo founders. *Everything* is theirs
to decide or change. The question is filtering for something that isn't the real problem.

**2. And this is the serious one — it hands the job back.** They came to us with a messy
project **asking for direction**. We check their input, decide it's not good enough, and
respond by asking them, in effect, *"so what do you think you should be working on?"*

That's the tool failing at the exact moment it claims to be rigorous. If they knew which
work mattered, they wouldn't need us. We're dressing up "we don't know" as a
sophisticated question.

## The constraint that makes this hard

We genuinely **do** need something more from them — we're not being pedantic. If the only
material we have is "build the app, build the database, build the engine," then any
roadmap we generate is one we invented, not one derived from their situation. That is
precisely the failure the whole product exists to prevent.

So we can't just proceed. But we also can't ask them to do our job.

## The insight we think is right (challenge it if it isn't)

Gate 1's question works because it asks them to **DESCRIBE**, not to **ANALYSE**.

So Gate 2's question should also ask for a description of something they already know —
and *we* do the analysis on the answer. The classification (is this a lever? is this a
pile of tasks?) is our job, not theirs. They should never have to know what a "lever" is.

Our current best attempt:

> **"When you sit down to work on this, what do you actually spend your time on?"**

The reasoning: it's a report on their week, not a strategy. If they say "building
features," we can see that's an artifact pile. If they say "talking to customers and
tweaking the pitch," there's a lever in there. **We** classify it; they just describe.

Problems with our attempt, which we can see but haven't solved:
- Many of these founders **haven't started yet** — there is no "time you spend on it". A
  future-tense version ("what would you be spending your time on?") drifts back toward
  asking them to plan, which is the original sin.
- A founder can honestly answer **"building the app"** — which is a truthful description
  and still gives us no lever. Then we're stuck in the same place, having spent their
  patience.
- It may just be too vague to reliably produce the information we need.

## What we're asking you

**Write a better Gate 2 question** — or tell us the whole approach is wrong.

It must:
- be answerable by a non-technical solo founder **in one or two sentences**;
- ask them to **describe something they already know**, not to strategise, prioritise, or
  work out what matters (that's what they hired us for);
- reliably surface whether there's **ongoing, repeatable work that keeps paying off** —
  as opposed to a finite pile of tasks or things to build;
- work for founders who **haven't started building yet**;
- not blame them, and not imply they gave a bad answer.

Also worth telling us:
- Is the question even the right instrument here, or should we be extracting this from
  what they've already given us in some other way?
- Is Gate 2 a coherent thing to test for at all, or is the concept itself confused?
